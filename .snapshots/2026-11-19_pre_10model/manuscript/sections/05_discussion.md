# 4. Discussion

In the present study, we examined where the language of a Jacobian-lens readout comes from: the corpus the lens was fitted on, the model the lens is pointed at, or the two jointly. Each of twenty-three models, spanning five families (Qwen3, Qwen3.5, Qwen3.6, Qwen3.8, and Gemma-4) and a 53-fold range of scale, received two lenses fitted under one protocol, one on English WikiText and one on Chinese Wikipedia, the two corpora matched in window count, cut length and packing order, so that the contrast between them bundles language with script, vocabulary and content. Byte-identical probes were read through both lenses at every measured layer, and the candidate pools of each (probe, layer) cell were compared on composition, overlap, and readability.

Three findings emerged. First, both lenses placed the same concept at the same layers, and what they agreed on was not the string: most of the overlap between their candidate lists was carried by translations, with repeated tokens in the minority, and the agreement peaked in the deep half of the depth axis. Second, the language of the readout was set jointly. The fitting corpus shifted the composition of the pool, the Chinese band widening under the Chinese-fit lens in every model, but each model kept a preferred language that survived the lens being turned the other way (English for Gemma, Chinese for Qwen), family-stable from 0.6B to 32B. Third, blackouts belonged to the lens, and to the lens alone: cells that read as pure fragments under one lens carried ordinary words under the other, with the English-fit lens going dark while the Chinese-fit lens still read in 501 of 1548 cells, against 18 in the reverse direction.

Read together, the findings separate three properties of a lens readout: the language of the pool belongs to the fitting corpus (the text the lens was fitted on), darkness belongs to the lens-model pair, and the preferred language belongs to the model, whichever part of it carries it. None of the three is visible from one lens alone, and each appears only when the two are set against each other. Competition among surface forms is a documented property of model outputs (Holtzman et al., 2021), and an analogous competition between scripts operates on the readout side.

## 4.1 Language preference may come from the model

The lens side of this account is already settled: with the model, the probes and the readout procedure held fixed, a change of fitting corpus moves the readout in every model (Section 3.1).

The same dependence appears beside this design. Probing the same hidden states with three estimators yields three different latent-language distributions (Bayazit et al., 2026), and two Jacobian lenses that differ only in fitting language report different tokens for the same states (He et al., 2026). What remains is which part of the model carries the preference.

The first candidate is what the model was trained on. The preference tracked the family, and the two families document different pre-training data. Qwen3 reports 36 trillion tokens across 119 languages and dialects (Yang et al., 2025). The Gemma 4 report describes web, code, image and audio data (Gemma Team, 2026). Concordant evidence comes from models whose mixtures differ by design. Non-English-centric models activate multiple latent languages and select among them (Zhong et al., 2025), and balanced multilingual training reshapes internal language representations (Trinley et al., 2025). Continued pre-training turns the mixture into an explicit design variable: SEA-LION continued the pre-training of Llama-3.1-8B and Gemma-2-9B on roughly 200 billion tokens split 55% Southeast Asian languages, 25% English and 20% code, raising performance across eleven Southeast Asian languages while the retained English share limited the loss of English ability (Ng et al., 2025). A shift in data composition of this size changes which languages a model handles well without changing its architecture, which is the relation the present account requires between training mixture and preferred language.

The second candidate is the output vocabulary. The readout is projected through the model’s own unembedding, so a skew in that matrix, learned from the same training mixture, would also appear in the readout. The design separates the lens from the model, not the model’s parts, and nothing here rules that account out. Two observations leave room for the intermediate state as well: the composition of the pool changes with depth, and it changes with the fitting corpus. A fixed unembedding predicts neither. The output distribution itself gives a third observation: at the last layer the model’s own top-5 contains no Chinese candidate in any of the twenty-three models and four probes, while the Chinese-fit lens reads Chinese in 0.57 of the intermediate pool slots, 0.61 on the Qwen models and 0.34 on the Gemma ones. The pools carry language the output distribution does not, although this comparison does not isolate the unembedding itself.

The fitting corpus is a reportable attribute. A lens artifact that does not state the language, source, and size of its corpus is underspecified in a way that matters. Two readers using two corpora may report different candidates, ranks, and hit rates for the same model on the same prompt (see Appendix Tables A1 through A6), without any disagreement about the model. Reporting the corpus lets a reader separate those differences from differences in the model itself. Training data is reported this way for learned probes, and circuit discovery set the precedent for reporting datasets and metrics (Conmy et al., 2023).

The dependence extends beyond lenses. A readout is evidence about a model only jointly with the data that shaped the reader. On the intervention side, the same point appears as the distinction between steerability and decodability (Nadaf, 2026). On the feature side, it appears as English-only dictionaries yielding unreliable language steering (Al Ghussin et al., 2026; Chou et al., 2025).

## 4.2 The language itself may also contribute

A lens blackout shows the model’s contribution most directly. The hidden state is not empty: the English-fit lens returned punctuation and whitespace, and the Chinese-fit lens read ordinary words. At layer 18 of Qwen3-14B on the currency probe, the English-fit hundred held nothing but punctuation while the Chinese-fit lens read 什么样的 (“what kind of”), 吗 (“question particle”), 什么地方 (“what place”), 哪个 (“which one”), 当地人 (“local people”), the framing of a question about a place. That readable side is not a damaged pool: it carries the kind of material a pool readable under both lenses carries, function words and the vocabulary of the question. Readability is weak on both sides, since a readable pool is mostly function words under either lens, and the probe’s own content appears only where the answer family surfaces. The cells dark under both lenses, most of them on the sport and spider probes, mark a limit of this pair of lenses. Whether the model is empty in those cells remains to be shown, and a lens fitted on some third language would be expected to find words where these two find none.

The surviving language was Chinese in every family, including the family whose readout preference runs the other way. Within Gemma-4, the cells readable under exactly one lens ran 55 to 3 toward Chinese (Appendix Table A7). The distribution was also uneven across languages. A language-neutral concept space would place a concept at a comparable distance from all of its surface forms, and every lens would find its own script at similar depths and shares. Instead, each state concentrated where particular languages live (Section 3.1). A concept space of this kind cannot be separated from the structure of the languages themselves, because a state sits close to the surface forms of some languages and far from those of others.

Whether the language itself contributes, over and above the materials, is a hypothesis the present data cannot close, but it has precedents. In humans, the structure of a language shapes reasoning-relevant representation: children whose number words name place value transparently, 四十二 reading as four-ten-two, build base-ten representations earlier than English-speaking peers whose number words do not (Miura et al., 1988). Across languages, speech conveys information at roughly equal rates but at very different densities per unit, so no language is simply faster (Coupé et al., 2019), and on the model side the tokenization stage alone imposes up to fifteen-fold differences in how much text a token carries across languages (Petrov et al., 2023). Languages also differ in where the information sits. Comparing a Chinese and an English multimodal emotion-cause corpus, D. Wu et al. (2025) found the cause of an emotion carried by visual cues in 72.7% of sampled Chinese pairs against 39.1% of English ones, while text carried it at similar rates, a contrast they attribute to high- versus low-context communication. If the same content is distributed differently across channels in different languages, the surface forms through which a readout can find it will differ as well. Findings that Chinese-centric models reason more token-efficiently in Chinese than in English are contested and model-dependent (Ren et al., 2026). What the present results add to that open question is an asymmetry measured on the readout side: the readout can prefer one language while the content that survives is in another.

## 4.3 LLM thinks like a bilingual

All measurements were taken at intermediate layers, prior to the emission of any token, so the readouts concern internal computation rather than the language of the answer. The constructs used to interpret them come from research on the bilingual individual, in which the two positions of the Introduction have long been opposed, the language of thought taken as fixed by the history of acquisition or as moving with the present. In that literature the opposition is not exclusive, because both languages are present in the mind at once and any input activates the two together (the BIA+ model; Dijkstra & van Heuven, 2002; Marian & Spivey, 2003). In the Revised Hierarchical Model the second language reaches meaning through its links to the first at low proficiency, and the direct route to concepts strengthens as proficiency grows (Kroll & Stewart, 1994). Within this framework, the language of an episode of thought is a joint product of an acquisition history that establishes the dominant language and a present context that modulates it, each position in the debate holding for one of the two terms.

The first finding is direct. The candidate pool of an intermediate state holds candidates in both languages: the pools of the middle layers carry one concept across the two scripts, with translations co-ranked alongside same-language forms in a single list (Section 3.2). The readout parallels what introspective reports describe (Sawyer, 2016): two languages present in one state, reported from within and measured from without.

Four components of BIA+ and the RHM correspond to what is measured here: shared concepts to the overlap carried by translations (Section 3.2), language-specific forms to the two scripts of one pool, dominance to the family-stable preference (Section 3.1), and context-dependent access to the shift of composition under the other fitting corpus.

The second finding is the joint product, with each term measured separately. The default language behaves as the history term, carried by the model: it followed the training mixture, English for every model and Chinese wherever the mixture had supplied it (Section 3.1), the counterpart of first-language dominance in human inner speech, where the second language enters only as acquisition makes it strong (Resnik, 2021). The composition of the pool is the context term, carried by the lens, and it followed the fitting corpus in every model. This context term sits one level further out than in a bilingual, in whom the language of operation shifts the processing itself (Athanasopoulos et al., 2015): the lens leaves the processing fixed, and the Chinese content it surfaces was present in the activation all along, since a linear map can only re-weight what is there. The anchored-and-mobile synthesis of the Introduction is realized here as two measured quantities, each tied to a candidate source.

The third finding is the blackout asymmetry, and its counterpart is language-bound access to internal knowledge. Bilinguals retrieve internally stored content through the language it was built in: exact arithmetic trained in one language does not transfer to the other (Spelke & Tsivkin, 2001), and autobiographical memory returns in its language of encoding (Larsen et al., 2002). The readout shows the same binding from the outside: states in which the Chinese-fit lens reads ordinary words while the English-fit lens reads nothing ran 501 cells to 18 across the battery, and in the cells readable under exactly one lens it was Chinese that survived, in every family, Gemma included (Section 3.3). The content is present, and the channel is language-bound. The binding here sits one level further out as well, since it is the lens’s reading that is bound, where in the bilingual the binding is in retrieval itself.

He et al. (2026) reach a converging caution from a different instrument: decomposing the readout into sparse features, they find token-level lens scores corpus-conditional while the dominant features stay stable, so token-level claims about a model’s latent language, such as thinking in English, cannot be taken at face value. We agree in part. The present design cannot decide what the underlying mode of thinking is, whether non-English, non-monolingual, or purely semantic. The reason lies in the unit of measurement: the readout operates on whole tokens, and whole tokens are not informationally equivalent across languages. The particle 的 is bound and always attributive; the suffix of “-city” carries the category; 八, 8, and eight package one quantity three ways; the euro family spreads over euro, eur, euros, and EUR where Chinese spends one form, 欧元; “city” names a bare category where 这座城市 points at one city in a context; 法国 builds the country from the productive morpheme 国 (“country”), which also builds 中国 (“China”) and 美国 (“America”), where “France” is atomic. A readout that prefers one language is therefore choosing between non-equivalent packages of meaning. Nor can the level of the shared content be decided from here. Morpho-semantic cores are exactly what translation pairs share, as in the “called” family surfacing as llamado and chamado in one pool. English tokens surfacing alongside the Chinese answer under a single lens (Gurnee et al., 2026) are a one-lens instance of the same indeterminacy. We claim only what the data force: the shared content is no coarser than a concept.

What the preference data do show is that the purely semantic extreme is not supported. A form-free, language-neutral medium would not produce a family-stable preference, and it would not produce a blackout asymmetry between scripts. The mirror-image literature points the same way: holding the lens fixed and varying the prompt’s language surfaces English first (Wendler et al., 2024; Schut et al., 2025), while holding the prompt fixed and varying the lens surfaces the model’s own language. Readout language is thus a property of neither the prompt alone nor the lens alone, and it is not universally English. We suggest the question be framed through cross-language token efficiency. Languages package the same content into tokens at different rates (Petrov et al., 2023), and the running debate over Chinese reasoning efficiency is exactly about such rates (Ren et al., 2026). A model’s preferred language may be the one that packages what it is computing at the lowest token cost. The Qwen preference for Chinese fits that reading, and so, at the moment of blackout, does Gemma.

## 4.4 Future work

Several directions follow from the present findings. First, a broader probe battery would allow the preference to be ranked per model, replacing the two-direction design with a profile over many languages. A battery that crosses concreteness with loanword status would test the framework directly, since it predicts higher cross-language overlap for concrete items, the same property that lets concrete words serve as cross-lingual anchors when lexical-semantic variation between languages is modelled (X. Zhang et al., 2024); reversing the prompt language would separate the model’s preference from the prompt’s one. Second, more languages and more script classes should be tested. The mechanics observed here, a Han-script lens surfacing Han candidates against a Latin lens that reads everything from Spanish to fragments, will differ for scripts with other tokenization properties. The attribution of tokens to languages, being rule-based, inherits the same fragility: a token classed as another language on script grounds may in fact be a fragment. The 168 cells dark under both lenses are the natural target for a third-language lens, which the blackout analysis predicts will find words where these two find none. Third, two controls would sharpen the causal story: a within-language corpus pair (two English corpora matched in size and disjoint in content), separating the language of a corpus from its content, and a fixed-lens cross-lingual comparison (one lens read on prompts in both languages, with the candidate set restricted to each language’s tokens), testing the concept-sharing claim directly. A further control would hold architecture and vocabulary fixed and vary only the training mixture: a base model read alongside its continued-pre-trained descendant, such as Gemma-2-9B and Gemma-SEA-LION-9B (Ng et al., 2025), would test whether the preferred language moves with the data the model was trained on. Fourth, feature-level readout of the kind He et al. (2026) introduce would re-measure the preference below the token, answering whether it lives in the computation or in the token interface. Finally, the token-efficiency framing is quantifiable: per-language token costs measured on matched content could be correlated with preference strength across models, turning the closing hypothesis of Section 4.3 into a test.

The scope of these results is bounded in two ways. First, the battery is four probes, so the aggregate hit rates were computed over (probe, layer) cells and the per-probe patterns in the appendix are case studies, with too few items for estimates. The recovery rates are low partly by construction. The lens reads the whole vocabulary distribution of an intermediate state and is not tuned to answer extraction, and the smaller models fail the probes at their own final layers (a 0.50 prompt rate on Qwen3-0.6B and its six quantized variants, Qwen3-1.7B-BNB4, Qwen3-1.7B-Q4_K_M, and Qwen3.5-2B; 0.25 on Qwen3.5-0.8B), so no demand of answer correctness is placed on them, the value of the readout lying in the thinking path itself. Second, what the study measures is lens readout, not the model’s internal computation: no result here supports a claim about what a model experiences, or about states it could report if asked. The readout is a measurement instrument, and the reading is what has been characterized here.

Each side of the readout carries the script of its corpus. The English-fit pool is a Latin-script mixture, and English is one of its members. The Chinese-fit pool is Chinese, to within the Japanese residue counted in Section 2.3.
