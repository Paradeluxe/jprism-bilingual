# Abstract

Bilinguals hold two languages in one mind: acquisition history sets a native preference, and the task at hand sets a working language. We ask whether a language model, like a bilingual, has a preferred language of its own, and whether that preference is present in its unverbalized reasoning. We used the Jacobian lens (J-lens): it decodes intermediate activations into a distribution over the vocabulary through a linear map averaged over a fitting corpus. To date, reported lenses have been fitted on a single language, most often English, which leaves open whether a model’s English-dominant output comes from its training data or from the corpus the lens was fitted on. We fitted two of these lenses under an identical protocol, one on English WikiText and one on Chinese Wikipedia, for each of twenty-three models spanning five families (Qwen and Gemma) and 0.6B to 32B parameters. The Qwen3 family is included in full precision and under three- to eight-bit quantization. We applied both lenses to the same four English factual probes and compared the two readouts at every measured layer of each model. (1) Gemma models tend to produce predominantly English tokens under both lenses, while Qwen models shift toward Chinese under the Chinese-fit lens and keep English under the English-fit lens. (2) Semantically overlapping candidates were matched between lenses, mostly without order and across languages. (3) Readouts may go dark in the top-100 candidates, more often under the English-fit lens than under the Chinese-fit one. These results are consistent with language models having a preferred language by default and a working language that follows the question at hand during thinking, a pattern reported for bilingual speakers.

Keywords: Jacobian lens, mechanistic interpretability, multilingual representations, language preference, bilingualism
