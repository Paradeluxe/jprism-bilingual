# 1. Introduction

Bilingual speakers can communicate fluently in one language while largely excluding the other from their overt speech. Yet overt language use does not fully reveal the linguistic processes that produce it. Researchers can observe the language of the final response, but the involvement of different languages during internal processing is more difficult to determine. Addressing this problem requires indirect evidence from behavioral and neurocognitive measures, as well as self-reports of experienced inner speech.

Evidence from bilingual language processing indicates that the non-target language can remain active even when it is not overtly used. Words presented in the target language can activate their unpresented translation equivalents and related competitors in the non-target language (Blumenfeld & Marian, 2007; Shook & Marian, 2019; Thierry & Wu, 2007). Research on multilingual inner speech similarly suggests that both languages may be used internally. The first language often remains dominant, but use of the second language increases with proficiency, frequency of use, and naturalistic exposure (Resnik, 2021). These findings show that fluent performance in one language can coexist with the internal involvement of another.

Although multiple languages may be active during bilingual processing, they do not necessarily contribute equally. Instead, internal processing may be anchored more strongly to one language, with the anchor shaped by both prior experience and the current context. Autobiographical memories are more readily accessed in the language the events were encoded (Larsen et al., 2002), while exact arithmetic learned in one language transfers less readily to another than approximate number judgments do (Spelke & Tsivkin, 2001). These findings suggest that the language associated with learning or encoding can retain privileged access to the relevant information. However, this anchoring is not fixed. Color-category boundaries can shift with the language currently in use (Sinkeviciute et al., 2024), while duration judgments and motion-event categorization vary with the language of operation (Athanasopoulos et al., 2015; Bylund & Athanasopoulos, 2017). Thus, the language that most strongly organizes internal processing depends on both accumulated linguistic experience and the immediate language context. Consistent with this account, Sawyer (2016) concluded that bilingual inner speech can remain anchored to the language in which experiences were encoded while also changing with context and purpose. Fluent production in one language therefore does not by itself identify the language, or languages, that organize the processing preceding it.

Multilingual language models present a parallel measurement problem. Like multilingual human speakers, models can produce a fluent response in one language without revealing whether their intermediate processing is anchored to that language, another language, or a representation shared across languages. A model’s linguistic orientation may reflect its training experience, the current prompt context, or an interaction between the two. It may process the input primarily in the prompt language, rely on another language as an internal pivot, activate several languages simultaneously, or represent meaning in a form that is partly independent of any particular language.

Existing findings place models along a continuum between language-specific and language-independent processing. At one end, intermediate representations appear to favor a particular language, usually English. Under the logit lens, non-English inputs in Llama-2 models first surface English tokens, suggesting that the models operate in an English-centered latent space (Wendler et al., 2024). This preference is graded rather than categorical. Schut et al. (2025) found that nouns tend to surface in English, whereas other parts of speech remain in the prompt language. They also found that steering vectors constructed in English were more effective than those constructed in the target language. Language organization can also be localized at the component level: language-specific neurons can be identified and manipulated to alter language-related behavior (Tang et al., 2024; Kojima et al., 2024). At the level of written form, readouts based on romanized representations emerge before those tied to language-specific scripts (Saji et al., 2025). Together, these findings suggest that language organization varies across layers, linguistic components, and representational levels rather than reflecting a simple choice between English and the prompt language. English-centric anchoring is not confined to decoder models or to readout. In multilingual encoders, implicit cross-lingual alignment pulls the lexical semantics of other languages toward their English counterparts, so that language-specific shades of meaning, which are smaller for concrete than for abstract words, have to be recovered explicitly; modelling them improved emotion recognition in Spanish, Japanese and Chinese, with the larger gains in the two languages more distant from English (X. Zhang et al., 2024).

Other studies provide evidence for multilingual or partially language-general representations. Non-English-centric models activate multiple latent languages and select among them according to the source and target languages (Zhong et al., 2025). During translation, Aya-23 activates typologically related languages, whereas more English-centric models rely more strongly on a single pivot language (Trinley et al., 2025). At the level of meaning, semantically similar inputs occupy nearby regions of a shared representational space across languages and modalities (Z. Wu et al., 2025). The available evidence therefore does not place model processing at either a purely language-specific or a fully language-independent pole. More importantly, these findings depend on how intermediate representations are measured.

Intermediate model states are not directly readable. Claims about their linguistic content therefore depend on an interpretability method that transforms, decomposes, or intervenes on those states. One family maps intermediate activations onto distributions over the output vocabulary. The logit lens applies the model’s unembedding matrix directly to an intermediate state (Nostalgebraist, 2020). The tuned lens instead learns a layer-specific affine transformation that aligns each intermediate state with the final-layer distribution, producing readouts that are more predictive, reliable, and less biased than those of the untuned lens (Belrose et al., 2023). However, information that affects behavior can remain undecodable under the logit lens (Nadaf, 2026), and latent-language readouts can vary across estimators and fitting choices (Bayazit et al., 2026). A second family uses sparse autoencoders to decompose activations into dictionaries of features. The resulting dictionaries depend on the text on which they are trained. English-only dictionaries preferentially encode monolingual structure and may provide unreliable control over multilingual behavior (Chou et al., 2025; Al Ghussin et al., 2026). A third family intervenes on model components to identify causal contributions to behavior. Activation patching and automated circuit-discovery methods edit, replace, or ablate components to test whether they carry a target behavior (Conmy et al., 2023). Their conclusions nevertheless depend on methodological choices, including the evaluation metric and the corruption procedure used for patching (F. Zhang & Nanda, 2024). These approaches answer related but distinct questions. Vocabulary-based lenses identify information that can be decoded in vocabulary space, sparse dictionaries identify features associated with behavior, and intervention methods identify components that causally contribute to behavior.

Among these approaches, tuned lenses and sparse feature dictionaries are fitted on corpora. For these tools, evidence suggests that their behavior depends on the data used for fitting. Bayazit et al. (2026) identified the tuned-lens fitting corpus as one probe-specific choice that could shift latent-language measurements, but they did not manipulate the fitting language directly. The closest precedent is Indic-TunedLens, which fitted a single shared lens on a mixture of 11 Indian languages and showed that English-centric lenses decode Indic scripts poorly (Panchal et al., 2026). This finding demonstrates that the linguistic composition of the fitting data matters as the lens was fitted on a multilingual mixture. The study, however, did not isolate the effect of fitting-corpus language through a matched comparison of monolingual lenses. More broadly, previous studies have changed the estimator, combined multiple fitting languages, or compared lenses that differed in more than one respect. None has directly manipulated the language of the fitting corpus while holding the model states, fitting protocol, and evaluation probes constant.

The Jacobian lens, introduced alongside the J-space framework, is constructed by averaging the model’s Jacobian over a fitting corpus (Gurnee et al., 2026). This procedure produces a fixed, context-independent linear map from each source layer to the output space. When applied to an intermediate activation, the map generates a distribution over the output vocabulary that approximates the model’s latent prediction at that depth. Each output token is associated with a J-lens vector. In the original work, these vectors also supported interventions: replacing the vector associated with France with the vector associated with China shifted downstream answers accordingly. The present study uses only the lens’s readout function. The representations surfaced by the lens are collectively termed the J-space. Gurnee et al. (2026) argued that these representations exhibit several functional properties associated with a global workspace: their content can be reported, deliberately elicited and maintained, used during intermediate steps of silent reasoning, and made available to downstream computations. By contrast, some automatic processes, including aspects of text parsing, may proceed without entering this space (Gurnee et al., 2026; Goyal et al., 2022). Under this interpretation, the Jacobian lens provides a potentially useful window into information available for downstream computation and verbalization, including intermediate content that does not appear in the final output. The logit and tuned lenses test what can be decoded in vocabulary space, sparse dictionaries identify learned features associated with behavior, and intervention methods identify components that causally support behavior. Within the J-space framework, the Jacobian lens is interpreted as surfacing intermediate information that is available for subsequent computation and possible verbalization. The original J-space study illustrates its latent-language interpretation with a Chinese prompt. When asked for the antonym of 小 (“small”), the model produces 大 (“big”), while the intermediate J-lens readout contains the English tokens big and bigger. Gurnee et al. (2026) suggested that the model may, in some sense, “think in English” in its intermediate layers. However, the English tokens could reflect the model, the lens, or their interaction. As the Jacobian lens is fitted on a corpus, the language of that corpus may influence which vocabulary items become most visible in its readout. More broadly, Bayazit et al. (2026) showed that different instruments applied to the same hidden states can produce different latent-language distributions. They concluded that claims about a model’s latent language are conditional on the instrument used to measure it. Tools from different methodological families differ in their objectives, transformations, fitting procedures, and inductive biases. Their disagreement cannot therefore be attributed specifically to fitting-corpus language. A stronger test requires two lenses of the same kind, fitted under the same protocol and applied to the same hidden states, but fitted on corpora in different languages. Including both English and Chinese prompts also allows corpus-language preferences to appear in either direction, rather than testing only whether Chinese processing produces English readouts.

The present study compares an English-fitted and a Chinese-fitted Jacobian lens to identify which properties of their readouts vary with the fitting corpus and which remain stable across lenses. For each of 23 models from five families, ranging from 0.6B to 32B parameters, we fitted one lens on English WikiText and another on Chinese Wikipedia. For each model, the two lenses followed the same fitting protocol: 1,000 sequences of 128 tokens, the model’s tokenizer for both corpora, identical source-layer sampling, the same numerical precision, and the same skip rules. We then applied both lenses to the same four factual probes at the same layers and token positions and compared their readouts directly. The models span a 53-fold range in scale, allowing corpus-dependent and corpus-invariant patterns to be examined within and across model families. This broader comparison tests whether apparent lens effects are stable across model families and scales or specific to particular model-lens combinations.

The present study addresses three questions. First, when both lenses produce interpretable readouts, do they recover the same underlying concept? If so, is their agreement expressed through whole-word tokens, semantically corresponding tokens, or related subword fragments? Second, when the fitting corpus favors one language, to what extent does the readout follow that language? Conversely, when both lenses reveal the same language despite their different fitting corpora, is this corpus-invariant pattern consistent with a stronger contribution from the model than from the fitting corpus? We examine the direction and stability of these patterns across model families and scales. Third, when one lens produces only uninterpretable fragments at a particular layer, does the other lens fail at the same layer? Shared failure would suggest that the relevant information is not recoverable by either lens, whereas asymmetric failure would indicate that readout availability depends on the specific combination of model and fitting corpus. Together, these analyses address the provenance of language in intermediate readouts. Before a language-specific pattern observed through a fitted lens is attributed to the model, it is necessary to determine how strongly that pattern depends on the corpus used to construct the lens.
